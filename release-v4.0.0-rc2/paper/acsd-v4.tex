\begin{abstract}
Anonymous scholarly releases may change authors, acquire new versions, and
disclose selected identities or research records after publication. Signatures,
timestamps, and archives supply essential building blocks, but their composition
must specify who authorizes each transition, which signatures existed by a
given time, and what a later disclosure establishes. We present \acsd, an
executable protocol profile for these decisions. A jointly signed target binds
the release, author governance, evidence policy, and exact lineage transition.
A separate approval set closes over the completed signatures before timestamping.
Predecessor authority controls succession, including a precommitted recovery
path; typed evidence rules constrain partial disclosure and derived claims.
Lean proves target and transition non-reuse, required authorization, and scope
non-amplification, with executable transcript checkers connecting verified
adapter facts to the model. Evaluation combines adversarial lifecycle cases,
independent implementation checks, a scripted three-author coordination run,
and 90 measured local creation/verification samples comparing ACSD, detached
Ed25519, and actual GnuPG. A manual signed
transition is a successful authorization control. ACSD adds explicit composition
rules and their verification at measurable storage and runtime cost.
The resulting evidence supports pseudonymous assent and authorized evolution;
historical authorship and the truth of scientific contributions require
separate assessment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Researchers often want to disclose a manuscript before conventional publication while keeping the author identity unavailable during review. A common informal proposal is to upload an anonymous file, sign it, and rely on repository history. That proposal leaves several distinct questions entangled.

One concrete trigger is the interval around an arXiv submission: a first-time or
new-category submission may require endorsement, while moderation and review
are separate from scientific peer review. During that interval an author may
want a public, content-anonymous evidence package that preserves exact bytes
and team assent without pretending to replace arXiv's process. We use this as
an application scenario, not as a claim that arXiv queues have a measured
backlog or that ACSD should circumvent its rules.

\paragraph{Why signatures and timestamps do not specify the workflow.}
Consider three pseudonymous authors who release a manuscript, replace one team
member in its next version, and later disclose only two names. GPG can verify
each signature, OpenTimestamps can witness a committed object's time, and
Zenodo can archive a release. These operations do not specify whether the old
team authorized the replacement, whether the time evidence covers every
required approval, or whether two disclosures justify a full-byline claim.
An attacker can sign an apparent next version under a fresh key and obtain
valid time evidence for it while none of those operations establishes
predecessor authorization. The missing contract is the relation among signed
objects, authorized transitions, and permitted conclusions.

\acsd\ (Anonymous Scholarly Claim and Disclosure) makes that contract explicit.
The authoring path binds a release, its governance statement, its
\emph{Provenance Evidence Capsule} (PEC), and an exact successor transition to
one approval target. Verification requires the applicable author authority and
checks each disclosed claim against evidence of the correct type and scope.
An independent RFC~3161 timestamp can then cover the completed signature set.
The public content may omit names while hosting accounts and reused keys
remain linkable.

Ordinary cryptographic components can implement these rules. Our manual
predecessor-signature control confirms that an explicitly constructed transition
can reproduce the core succession decision. That control tests the effect of
adding the authorization rule; it is not functionality supplied automatically
by detached signing or archival publication. The contribution is the protocol
design, its attack-exclusion properties, and an executable implementation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An explicit composition protocol.}
  Joint approval binds manuscript, governance and evidence policy; a second
  acyclic object binds completed signatures before timestamping. Exact
  predecessor authorization governs revisions and precommitted recovery.
  Optional series objects allow mutual citation without circular content hashes.
  \item \textbf{Formal attack-exclusion results.}
  Lean proves that approvals and transitions cannot be reused for different
  targets, accepted successors require the applicable authority, and evidence
  cannot grant claims beyond its permitted type and exact scope. Executable
  transcript checking and refinement connect these rules to adapter outputs.
  These statements quantify over the modeled inputs, beyond the finite
  adversarial cases exercised by tests.
  \item \textbf{An evaluated reference implementation.}
  A command-line workflow supports team approval, authorized revision, recovery,
  distributed approval collection, exact-target delegation to a coordinator
  agent, and partial/full identity disclosure. Independent verifiers, adverse
  transcripts, a successful manual authorization control, actual GnuPG
  comparisons and a real timestamp receipt make its behavior and costs
  reproducible.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Architecture and Evidence Flow}
\label{sec:architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} separates object construction, external evidence
checking, and the formal claim boundary. The release digest fixes manuscript
bytes and version metadata. Governance fixes author roles and declarations.
The PEC commits evidence events and policy. For a successor, the transition
also fixes the parent, child and proposed authority. All required keys approve
the same target; finalization then fixes the exact signature set.
These two stages distinguish assent to an object from evidence that the
completed assent existed by a particular time.

Later timestamp receipts, dialogue openings and identity disclosures are
separately checked against their original subjects. The adapter checks produce
facts for the claim layer. The Lean model proves how those facts may compose,
and the strict transcript checker reconstructs the corresponding derivations.
The adapter boundary and model assumptions are explicit; Section~\ref{sec:formal}
states the principal security properties.

\section{Scope and Threat Model}

\paragraph{Assets.} A release contains manuscript bytes, an ordered list of anonymous author keys, roles and contribution declarations, an AI-use declaration, and optional parent-release information. A series package contains references to completed releases, a series key, sequencing information, and cross-paper semantic relations. Public keys are pseudonymous identifiers.

\paragraph{Adversary.} We consider a party that can copy public content,
generate fresh keys, reuse a WorkID and predecessor digests, or claim the next
unused version. The party may reorder or substitute unsigned metadata,
timestamp a target before adding signatures, replay a disclosure or transition
under another scope, substitute an uncommitted recovery guardian, combine
ordinary and recovery authorizations, or present a partial byline as complete.
It may also reuse a key across works, submit signed but malformed JSON, claim
unsupported cross-paper citations, or cause a series key to sign incompatible
objects. Finally, a malicious or compromised author-slot key may sign a false
identity string naming an uninvolved person. We assume neither that signatures
reveal human identities nor that contribution declarations are true, recovery
guardians remain honest or available, or local timestamps are trusted.

\paragraph{Non-goals.} \acsd\ does not prove authorship by a natural person, independent discovery, plagiarism, venue acceptance, peer review, or legal nonrepudiation. These are not merely unimplemented features: some require social, institutional, or legal evidence outside the cryptographic object. The profile instead preserves evidence that can be useful in a later dispute.

\section{Security Goals and Trust Boundary}
\label{sec:security-goals}

The protocol is designed around four acceptance properties rather than a
generic claim that a signature proves authorship.  Let $A(x,E)$ mean that the
profile accepts object or conclusion $x$ from evidence set $E$.

\paragraph{G1: successor-authorization soundness.}
If a child release is accepted as the successor of a given predecessor, $E$
must contain an ordinary authorization by the applicable predecessor authority
or an authorization by the exact recovery authority committed in advance.
Knowing the WorkID, choosing the next version number, and signing under a fresh
key are insufficient.

\paragraph{G2: exact-transition non-reuse.}
Authorization for a transition binds its parent, child, proposed authority and
transition mode.  Evidence accepted for one child cannot authorize a distinct
child, even when both children reuse the same visible slot metadata.

\paragraph{G3: scope and type preservation.}
Every derived conclusion must be permitted by the closed policy and supported
by verified evidence of the required type for the exact subject.  In
particular, one author's identity statement cannot disclose another slot, an
event opening cannot establish identity, and a partial byline cannot silently
become a full byline.

\paragraph{G4: completed-assent time integrity.}
Time evidence over an unsigned target establishes, at most, target existence.
The stronger conclusion that the required approvals existed by a stated time
requires evidence over the closed approval-set object and the applicable
external-time checks.

These properties assume collision-resistant object identifiers, unforgeable
signatures for uncompromised keys, correct results from the explicitly named
adapters, and faithful evaluation of the model's predicates.  The Lean
development proves composition and attack exclusion above those inputs; it
does not re-prove cryptographic primitives, general PKIX validation, filesystem
integrity, or the honesty of a timestamp authority.  Compromised authorized
keys can issue protocol-valid false statements within their scope.  This
boundary is intentional: the system makes such residual authority visible
rather than translating it into an unsupported natural-person or originality
claim.

\section{Release Layer}

\subsection{Canonical release object}

Each \emph{PaperRelease} has a random stable \workid, a visible slot $(\workid,\textit{version},\textit{line})$, a content path, content length and digest, ordered author records, declared roles, contributions, an AI-use declaration, optional exact predecessor, and a list of citation witnesses. The release identifier is
\[
  \releaseid = \texttt{urn:sha256:}H(\mathrm{canonical}(R)).
\]
The content digest is over exact manuscript bytes. The release object uses a deliberately restricted JSON encoding: recursively sorted ASCII object keys, no insignificant whitespace, and safe integers only. A verifier parses, re-canonicalizes, and compares bytes before accepting a signature. This is a profile rule, not a claim that it is a general JSON standard.

\subsection{Joint approval target and closed capability calculus}

The \emph{AuthorshipGovernanceStatement} is the exact declaration of the work
id, manuscript digest, ordered byline slots and roles, corresponding-author key,
and AI-use field. The reference builder derives roles from the team configuration and release;
there is no privileged coordinator whose unsigned word makes it authoritative.
Instead, every declared author key approves its digest through the common
target described below. This proves assent to the declaration, not the factual
truth of its roles or contribution history.

The command-line builder accepts repeatable AI tool and purpose declarations
and one-based author slots recording human review. It rejects a partial
declaration that supplies a tool without a purpose or vice versa. These fields,
like contribution terms, are approved inside the common target. They remain
author assertions: the package preserves what the team approved but does not
independently establish that the declaration is exhaustive or factually true.
Fine-grained links from selected dialogue events to particular version changes
remain a separate disclosure workflow.

A \emph{Provenance Evidence Capsule} (PEC) binds the release subject and
governance digest to a digest-linked evidence-event sequence and a claim
policy. The policy contains a fixed-vocabulary list of permitted outcomes,
their required capabilities, and mandatory non-claims. A PEC also names one
team key as an issuer for provenance, but that label alone grants no outcome:
the complete team must still approve the common target.

The current command-line profile constructs an acyclic
\texttt{acsd-approval-target/v2} object. It points to already complete objects
and is itself signed only through detached approval envelopes. The following
abbreviated schema excerpt shows its fields; angle-bracketed values are
presentation placeholders rather than an accepted encoding:

\begingroup\scriptsize
\begin{verbatim}
{
 "schema":"acsd-approval-target/v2",
 "work_id":"urn:uuid:<work-id>",
 "release_digest":"<sha256>",
 "governance_digest":"<sha256>",
 "pec_digest":"<sha256>",
 "required_key_ids":["<key-id>"],
 "lineage_transition_digest":null
}
\end{verbatim}
\endgroup

Every required key issues a tagged COSE Sign1 signature over the same
canonical target bytes. Verification recomputes all four bindings and each key
id from the supplied public key. The closed capability calculus is the rule
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathrm{Granted}(P,o) \iff{}& \mathrm{Accepted}(P) \\
 &{}\land o\in P.\mathrm{permittedOutcomes} \\
 &{}\land \mathrm{CapabilityEstablished}(P,o).
\end{aligned}
\]
Policy permission is therefore only an upper bound; it is not evidence. The
outcome vocabulary is closed and versioned, so an unknown social claim
cannot enter the rule by adding a string to JSON.

Without the joint target, suppose authors sign only the release while an
attacker later changes the unsigned governance object or PEC policy. The
attacker could relabel roles, add the external-time outcome, or try to map a
local clock to that outcome while the old release signatures still verify.
Under ACSD, changing the governance or policy changes its digest and causes a
target-binding failure; recomputing the target instead causes the old COSE
approvals to fail their payload check. Even an approved policy entry yields no
outcome without its separately verified capability. A verifier can thus state
that the pseudonymous keys jointly approved this exact tuple, but cannot infer
who controls them without separate enrollment evidence.

The approval target is deliberately unsigned data until its detached
signatures arrive. Timestamping it at that stage would establish only
\emph{target existence}: an attacker who later obtains a key could append a
valid approval after the recorded time. Finalization therefore constructs an
\texttt{acsd-approval-set/v1} object containing the target digest and the
\sha\ digest of every exact author-approval COSE byte string, plus every
separate predecessor-authorization signature used for an authority change.
Only this closed approval-set object is the timestamp subject. Replacing, adding, or
removing a signature changes the approval-set digest. This is an evidence
closure rule, not a new signature or timestamp primitive.

\subsection{Authorized succession rather than version-number possession}

An exact predecessor digest proves structural continuity, not authority. A
fresh-key attacker can copy a public WorkID, name the real predecessor, choose
the consecutive version number, and validly sign the resulting child with the
attacker's keys. No hash or slot-conflict check answers whether those keys were
entitled to create the parent-to-child edge. The current profile therefore binds every
release to a \emph{lineage authority}: a sorted author-key set and a threshold
for authorizing the next edge. The default threshold is unanimity. It is stored
in the predecessor, so a child cannot lower it unilaterally.

Let $P$ be an accepted predecessor, $C$ a proposed child, $A_P$ the authority
bound by $P$, and $A_C$ the authority proposed by $C$. The verifier accepts a
lineage edge only if
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathrm{AuthorizedSuccessor}(P,C) ={}& \mathrm{ExactEdge}(P,C)\\
 &{}\land \mathrm{Authorization}(P,C).
\end{aligned}
\]
For unchanged authority, authorization is
\[
 \mathrm{Authorization}(P,C)=
 \mathrm{Quorum}_{P}(\mathrm{ApproveTarget}(C)),
\]
where $\mathrm{Quorum}_{P}$ applies the author set and threshold fixed by $P$.
For changed authority, let $T_{P,C}=\mathrm{Transition}(P,C)$; then
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathrm{Authorization}(P,C) ={}&
   \bigl(\mathrm{Quorum}(A_P,T_{P,C})\\
 &{}\lor \mathrm{RecoveryQuorum}(R_P,T_{P,C})\bigr)\\
 &{}\land \mathrm{AllApprove}(A_C,C).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \textsf{ExactEdge} requires the same WorkID, exact parent release and PEC
digests, and either a same-line increment by one or a new named line beginning
at version one. \textsf{Transition} binds both authorities and the exact child
release, governance, and PEC digests. The child approval target binds the
transition digest. The construction is acyclic because the transition refers
to complete child bodies while detached approvals refer to their completed
digests.

For an unchanged authority object, the ordinary child approvals already constitute
predecessor authorization and no duplicate signing step is needed. A key-set or
threshold change uses double control: the predecessor threshold signs the exact
transition and every new author signs the child target. Here $R_P$ is optional:
the predecessor may commit a sorted, nonempty recovery-key set disjoint from
$A_P$, together with its threshold. A recovery quorum can replace---not join---
the online predecessor quorum only for an authority-changing child, and every
child author still approves the exact child target. This resembles the
old-and-new authorization pattern used for TUF root-key updates, but ACSD
applies it to a single scholarly lineage edge rather than software repository
metadata~\cite{tufspec}. A series key may advertise a verified child as a head;
it cannot manufacture predecessor-author assent by itself.

Consider the concrete n+1 attack. Mallory constructs a child with P's WorkID,
P's exact release and PEC digests, the next version number, Mallory's public
key, and a valid Mallory signature over every child-controlled object. The
object is internally signed but has neither an unchanged predecessor authority
nor a transition signed by $A_P$. The implementation returns
\texttt{VALID\_OBJECT\_BUT\_UNAUTHORIZED\_SUCCESSOR}. It does not accuse
Mallory of plagiarism; it rejects only the claimed lineage edge.

This conclusion is relative to an accepted exact predecessor, not to the
WorkID string alone. The CLI can pin a previously accepted parent with
\texttt{--expected-parent-release-id}; otherwise it reports
\texttt{UNPINNED\_EXACT\_PARENT}. Without an external parent anchor, an isolated
package proves only that the authority named by its embedded parent authorized
the edge; it cannot distinguish a separately fabricated genesis that copied a
public WorkID.

Threshold choice exposes a real safety--liveness tradeoff. Unanimity prevents
one coauthor from transferring the lineage alone but lets one lost or dissenting
key freeze it. A lower threshold improves availability and is therefore allowed
only when committed by the predecessor. A recovery set is a second, prospective
tradeoff: it is public and linkable, must be committed before loss, and can
authorize one exact authority-changing transition, but it cannot create a
post-hoc identity recovery rule or revoke a valid past authorization. Without a
precommitted recovery authority, lost threshold keys safely stop the
cryptographic lineage. Two valid distinct children in the same exclusive slot,
including one online-authorized child and one recovery-authorized child, are
reported as equivocation with no automatic winner; choosing by observation time
requires an external transparency or governance policy.

\subsection{Citation witnesses}

The prototype recognizes only literal occurrences of a canonical token \texttt{urn:uuid:<work-id>} in the UTF-8 manuscript bytes. For every occurrence it records and signs
\[
  (\workid,\ \textit{byteOffset},\ \textit{byteLength}).
\]
The published \texttt{reference\_work\_ids} list must equal the ordered de-duplicated projection of these witnesses. This prevents a series package from inventing a citation edge that has no corresponding token in its source release, and prevents a copied text from silently relabeling an old target as a new \workid.

The rule intentionally does not parse PDFs, infer references from natural language, or determine whether a citation is intellectually appropriate. It is an exact byte-binding contract, not a semantic understanding claim.

For example, one UTF-8 source line contains the following token:
\begin{center}
\path{urn:uuid:12345678-1234-1234-1234-123456789abc}
\end{center}
The witness records that WorkID, the byte offset of the first character of the
token, and its byte length (45 bytes for this token). An editor that inserts a
character before the token must regenerate the witness. This is intentionally
mechanical: a source-level helper or build macro can insert the token, but v1
does not yet provide such editor integration, and hiding the token in a PDF
would defeat this scanner.

A minimal release fragment is conceptually:
\begin{verbatim}
{"slot":{"line":"main","version":1,
         "work_id":"urn:uuid:..."},
 "content":{"sha256":"..."},
 "reference_work_ids":["urn:uuid:..."],
 "citation_witnesses":[{
   "work_id":"urn:uuid:...",
   "byte_offset":35,"byte_length":45}]}
\end{verbatim}
The ellipses above are presentation placeholders only. The released demo
fixture binds a real manuscript's bytes, digest, and work identifier (the
governance and PEC objects are real, not placeholders), using synthetic author
identifiers. Real series releases are produced by the v1 fixture generator.

\subsection{Two graphs, two purposes}

A paper remains verifiable after all series packages are removed. The optional series layer records relationships among independently complete releases. A signed series package contains
\[
\begin{aligned}
  B_k = \mathrm{Sign}_{S}(&\textit{seriesId},\textit{epoch},\textit{sequence},\\
                          &\textit{previousBundleHash},\textit{rotationHash},\textit{rank},\\
                          &\textit{releases},\textit{heads},\textit{semanticEdges}).
\end{aligned}
\]

The semantic graph has vertices \workid s and may contain $P \rightarrow Q$ and $Q \rightarrow P$. Its edges express declared citation or related-work relations. The commitment graph has series anchors and completed \releaseid s. It does not make a release depend on a future series package. Thus two authors can cite stable random \workid s before both final documents are content-addressed; a later package resolves each \workid to a completed \releaseid. The semantic graph may be cyclic while the commitment graph remains a DAG.

The package includes a signed structural rank strictly above every release version it resolves. Combining anchor-to-release edges with exact release-to-parent edges decreases this rank along a commitment path. This rank is a graph certificate, not a timestamp, publication date, or priority claim.

The package fields have narrow roles: \textit{epoch} names the active series
key generation; \textit{rotationHash} commits its authorized key transition;
\textit{heads} maps each WorkID to its advertised line head; and
\textit{semanticEdges} records declared citation or related-work relations.
The rank is only a finite measure certifying that the commitment graph has no
cycle.

\subsection{Revisions, branches, and conflicts}

A revision names one exact predecessor. Multiple children of a parent are allowed, because a main manuscript and a computational appendix can be legitimate independent lines. A paper-level equivocation is instead detected when two valid, different releases occupy the same exclusive slot. A series-level equivocation is detected when two valid packages share the same series, epoch, sequence, and predecessor hash while committing to different contents.

An adversary can copy the content bytes, issue a fresh \workid and keys, and obtain a valid new lineage. \acsd\ classifies this as content reuse under a different lineage, not as a cryptographic proof of plagiarism. That restraint is important: reuse may be authorized, collaborative, or independently derived.

As a concrete attack, suppose an attacker copies P-v1, changes its WorkID,
and asks the series package to claim that it cites Q. The release verifier
recomputes the content digest and byte witnesses; if the copied source does not
contain the declared Q token, the package is rejected as an unsupported edge.
If the attacker copies the token too, the package can still be valid under a
new lineage. The evidence then says only “content reuse under a different
lineage”; deciding plagiarism or authorization remains outside the verifier.

\section{Scoped Post-release Statements}

\paragraph{Research-process evidence.}
Version lineage and committed dialogue records can preserve complementary
parts of a research history: a release fixes the manuscript at one stage,
while a later opening exposes selected records already committed by that
stage's PEC. For example, authors may reveal the dialogue behind an early
conditional result while keeping unrelated conversations private, then use
a later version for a stronger result and its new supporting material.
Exact event and release bindings prevent moving an opening to a different
subject. They establish neither that the opened window is representative of
the complete research process nor that contrary committed material was also
opened. An author can therefore construct a selective but cryptographically
valid narrative by revealing only favorable windows. Whether the disclosed
reasoning scientifically supports the stated result, and whether omitted
context changes that assessment, remain reviewable content questions. The current prototype supplies
the commitment and disclosure checks; automatic capture of research history,
AI-use declarations and contribution-to-event mappings remains incomplete.

Some evidence is necessarily published after the immutable release: authors
may open a committed dialogue window or reveal one name after review. Treating
these as arbitrary metadata would reintroduce policy amplification. ACSD uses
a small common rule for a statement $E$ of type $k$, exact subject $s$, signer
$a$, and requested claim $c$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathrm{Grant}(E,c) \Rightarrow{}& \mathrm{SignatureValid}(E) \\
 &{}\land \mathrm{ScopeValid}(E,s)
 \land \mathrm{Authorized}(k,s,a) \\
 &{}\land c\in\mathrm{Permitted}(k)
 \land \mathrm{Compatible}(k,c).
\end{aligned}
\]
This shared skeleton is intentionally not a general policy language. Event,
identity, and time evidence retain different schemas, authorization principals,
and proof predicates.

For a dialogue event, the verifier derives the required signer set from the
already approved PEC rather than trusting identifiers listed by the disclosure.
It then checks the disclosure policy, exact PEC/event binding, contiguous
Merkle opening, public-key identifiers, and every required COSE signature as
one acceptance decision. A valid opening detached from its policy or a valid
signature over a different opening grants nothing.

The slot-key identity assertion is an external per-slot sidecar binding the exact
\releaseid, WorkID, author slot, slot key, identity assertion, fixed purpose,
and optional publication reference. Its result is deliberately named
\texttt{SLOT\_KEY\_ASSENT\_TO\_IDENTITY\_ASSERTION}: it says that the release
slot's pseudonymous key signed the mapping, not that a natural-person identity
or venue acceptance was independently verified. In particular, possession of
a stolen slot key is sufficient to create a protocol-valid assertion naming an
uninvolved person. The verifier therefore exposes the narrow key-assent label
and the unsupported natural-person claim; consumers must not render the result
as authenticated civil identity without separate identity evidence. One slot can be revealed
without revealing another. A full-byline result requires a valid sidecar for
every release slot; conflicting assertions for one slot have no protocol-
selected winner.

The aggregate verifier labels any proper valid subset
\texttt{PARTIAL\_BYLINE\_KEY\_ASSENT}; it returns
\texttt{FULL\_BYLINE\_KEY\_ASSENT} only after quantifying over every slot in
the accepted release. An optional fail-incomplete mode lets a publication
workflow require full coverage without denying that partial disclosure can be
intentional. A common signed publication-reference string can crosswalk the
released bytes to a later record, but it remains an author-key assertion: it
does not derive venue acceptance. Extra contribution fields are rejected
rather than reinterpreted as contribution evidence.

Selective disclosure is cryptographically scoped but not socially isolating.
Revealing two members of a three-person team may permit inference about the
remaining slot from public collaboration networks, so a partial byline is not
an anonymity guarantee for undisclosed authors. Publication is also practically
irreversible: neither a later contradictory sidecar nor a protocol status can
make observers forget an identity string, and the signature alone cannot reveal
whether assent resulted from error, coercion, or key compromise. Authors should
therefore treat identity and dialogue disclosure as deliberate publication,
not as a reversible account-setting operation.

Exact sidecar scope also does not create cross-paper unlinkability. Reusing the
same public key in two unrelated WorkIDs exposes an equality edge even though a
sidecar for one release cannot be replayed against the other. The read-only
key-reuse audit reports this distinction; generating an independent key per
unrelated lineage is the privacy-preserving default, not a proof against all
traffic, account, or timing linkage.

\subsection{Submission and unblinding lifecycle}

A venue decision is deliberately not an ACSD state transition. Rejection can
require no public action, leaving the previously released bytes unchanged. If
review produces changed manuscript bytes, the submission becomes an exact
predecessor-authorized successor. If the accepted text is byte-identical and
only the byline is revealed, per-slot identity sidecars bind the existing
release directly; manufacturing a redundant n+1 version would conflate
identity disclosure with scientific revision. In either case, the authors may
sign a publication reference, but only independently authenticated venue
evidence could establish receipt or acceptance. Before publishing any sidecar,
each participating author should inspect its exact release, slot and assertion;
partial disclosure additionally requires a team-level privacy check because it
may expose non-participating coauthors by inference.

\section{Time Evidence}

Time is intentionally external to author signing. An RFC~3161 request contains
the \sha\ imprint of the frozen approval set. A response supports the limited
statement that the TSA signed a token for an imprint matching the locally
recomputed complete target-and-signature set no later than its recorded
\texttt{genTime}, only when the verifier supplies a TSA signer-certificate or
fingerprint pin independently of the package. A certificate and its fingerprint
copied into the same mutable package are not a trust anchor. The adapter checks
the request/response nonce, SHA-256 imprint, \texttt{id-ct-TSTInfo} content type,
single signer identifier, critical and exclusive timeStamping EKU, ESS
certificate identifier, signed attributes, and CMS signature. It implements an
exact-signer pin, not general PKIX path construction or revocation. Its local
test TSA is a protocol test and can never produce
\texttt{APPROVAL\_SET\_EXISTED\_NOT\_AFTER}.

There is no circularity: the timestamp receipt is a sidecar over the already
closed approval set. A later revision can bind the receipt hash if a new signed object
must refer to it. A timestamp does not prove creation at that moment,
authorship, originality, or global first discovery. Multiple independently
governed services can reduce single-service reliance, but their composition
requires a separate policy.

Legacy releases timestamped only the approval target. They retain a distinct
target-existence result and are never upgraded to an approval-set-time result.
This typed distinction closes a temporal-confusion attack while preserving
backward verification.

\section{Implementation and Evaluation}

The v1 prototype uses Python for deterministic fixture generation and a zero-dependency Node.js verifier for strict CBOR/COSE and profile validation; the two paths are intentionally separate. The package is designed for offline replay: locked dependencies are included, and the verification driver regenerates fixtures, verifies a standalone release, independently verifies the series profile, builds a manifest, and verifies that manifest.

A command-line tool (\texttt{acsd.py}) turns the profile into an executable
workflow. Core commands are \texttt{keygen}, \texttt{init},
\texttt{author-approve}, \texttt{finalize}, and \texttt{verify}. Portable
coauthor collection uses batch request/response directories and
\texttt{coordinator-finalize}; the latter can exercise exact-target
delegations addressed to supplied agent keys without receiving author private
keys or requiring copied author identifiers. Lineage uses \texttt{authorize},
\texttt{recover}, and \texttt{revise}; a separate \texttt{compare-successors} command detects
same-slot forks. Unblinding uses \texttt{disclose-identity} and
\texttt{verify-identity}; the aggregate command
\texttt{verify-identity-set} distinguishes partial from full coverage.
Finally, \texttt{audit-key-reuse} checks cross-work public-key equality. A
one-command \texttt{release} path covers a locally held key set. Approval
status is derived
from verified COSE files, never trusted from mutable state metadata. COSE uses
the registered EdDSA value $-8$, requires tag 18, and rejects non-minimal or
ambiguous CBOR encodings. A zero-dependency Node path independently verifies
Python-produced approvals. The canonical JSON layer is exercised by 64 fixed
Python--Node vectors plus 1,000 seeded generated cases; all generated cases
agree on canonical bytes and text-layer verdicts. Three known object-layer
float typing differences do not change the text-layer rejection result and
remain recorded in the canonical specification. The installable wheel and
immutable paper/evidence directory are separate artifacts; the authoritative
gate runs generated work in temporary directories and confirms source-tree
byte identity afterward.

The negative corpus includes the inherited rank, unsupported-edge,
citation-offset, canonical-JSON, and path attacks. New cross-layer regressions
replace a public key under an old key id; coherently replace governance and PEC;
recompute an unsigned target and attempt to reuse old approvals; alter COSE tags,
algorithm identifiers, map keys, and lengths; replace a timestamp and rewrite
the transport manifest; mismatch the nonce, signer, EKU, TSTInfo content type,
or signature algorithm; and attempt to promote the local test TSA. All are
rejected at their intended boundary. The single freeTSA result is an existence
check that the adapter can consume one real service response, not a reliability
study or a multi-provider conformance survey.

Additional regressions mutate approval-signature bytes after closure, attempt
to relabel a target-only timestamp as approval-set time, detach a valid dialogue
opening from its PEC or unanimous disclosure signatures, substitute a slot
key, replay an identity mapping to another release, and promote a partial
unblinding to a full byline. The deterministic demo now carries real Ed25519
release-approval and disclosure-approval signatures rather than self-reported
signer identifiers. Its verifier reconstructs the release approval target,
checks every required release approval, then verifies the separately scoped
event disclosure against the accepted PEC digest.

The local private-key profile is deliberately separate from the release wire
format. \texttt{keygen --encrypt} writes an encrypted PKCS\#8 Ed25519 PEM using
the maintained cryptographic library and prompts twice for a passphrase on an
interactive terminal. All signing commands share one loader: no passphrase is
accepted through an argument, environment variable, JSON value, or release
artifact, noninteractive encrypted-key use fails closed, and a one-command
release or revision prompts once per private-key path. The legacy plaintext PEM
mode remains for compatibility, but is documented as a lower local-protection
level rather than as a safe default. This protects a copied key file at rest;
it neither proves key custody nor substitutes for an OS keystore, hardware
signer, backup plan, or malware defense.

The executable semantic-confusion challenge applies four verified evidence
kinds to four claim kinds. Only the diagonal combinations are derivable; all
twelve cross-kind substitutions are denied. Three deliberately unsupported
social claims---natural-person identity, originality, and contribution
truth---remain unconstructible in the closed derivation table. A separate
timeline confirms that a target timestamp obtained before key compromise does
not establish that approvals added after compromise existed at that earlier
time.

The lineage corpus begins with the smallest discriminating attack: a child
with the exact WorkID and parent release/PEC digests, n+1, a fresh attacker
key, and complete self-approval. It is rejected at the missing
predecessor-quorum boundary. Positive cases cover unchanged authority, key
rotation, one-of-two and two-of-two thresholds, and a named branch; negative
cases cover threshold downgrade and transition-signature replay. Two valid
same-slot siblings yield equivocation with no winner.

The recovery corpus begins from a parent that committed two disjoint guardians
with a two-of-two threshold. Their signatures authorize one replacement-author
child only because the child changes online authority and every child author
approves its exact target. The runner rejects an uncommitted guardian, a parent
without a recovery commitment, an insufficient guardian quorum, a recovery
signature replayed to different child bytes, mixed online and recovery methods,
and recovery used merely to alter recovery configuration. A separately ordinary-
authorized and recovery-authorized child in the same slot remains a reported
fork with no winner. Thus recovery restores conditional liveness without
claiming guardian custody, retroactive revocation, complete fork visibility, or
an offline global head.

\subsection{Scripted distributed-author workflow}

We additionally execute the production CLI as four isolated roles: three
authors and one coordinator agent. The coordinator initializes an ordered
three-author candidate and exports one self-contained request per slot. Two
authors return direct approvals; the third signs a non-redelegable capability
for the exact target and the distinct coordinator-agent key. One transactional
\texttt{coordinator-finalize} invocation imports all responses, discovers the
delegated slot from that agent key, exercises the capability, and finalizes the
candidate. Offline verification then derives the narrower authorized-target
approval outcome for the mixed set.

After key setup, the happy path comprises seven CLI invocations: initialization,
batch export, three author responses, coordinator finalization, and
verification. It requires no JSON edits, no private-key transfer, and no manual
copying of a digest, WorkID, or key identifier. In an adverse run, the final
returned signature is changed and its transport manifest regenerated. Batch
import rejects the collection and leaves the live candidate with zero imported
approvals. The runner contacts no external service and uses an explicit
untimestamped test-only finalization policy.

This is a scripted role-based integration experiment with zero human
participants and zero production deployments. It demonstrates executable role
separation and transactional failure semantics; it does not establish first-time
human usability, adoption, or safe key-backup behavior.

To avoid a weak comparison, a controlled mechanism baseline fixes the signature

rotation and fresh-key capture; predecessor-key pinning rejects both. A manual

correctly accepts the rotation and rejects replay onto the attacker child.
ACSD reaches the same core decision. The positive control isolates the effect of explicit predecessor authorization.
The surrounding canonical object, threshold governance, approval closure and
typed claim rules specify the remaining application contract.

Three further executable workflows test post-release use. A two-author
submission lifecycle leaves a rejected release byte-identical, creates an
authorized successor only when manuscript bytes change, and binds an unchanged
release to a later publication reference without deriving acceptance. A
three-author unblinding run distinguishes 1/3 and 2/3 partial disclosure from
3/3 full coverage, and rejects duplicate slots, name mutation, and a smuggled
contribution field. A cross-paper run rejects sidecar replay across releases
while reporting that reuse of the same public key makes two WorkIDs publicly
linkable.

successor authority, recovery, completed-set time, or scoped disclosure from a
maintained package. For a one-off claim that selected keys signed immutable
bytes, detached signatures plus independently chosen time or archive evidence
are simpler and sufficient; ACSD should not be used merely to reproduce that
narrow statement.

defects in the underlying GPG signature algorithm.

had median 272~ms (SD~16); checking the ACSD approval-set binding as well had
median 295~ms (SD~28). Both use the same receipt parser and pinned signer;
these are incremental time-evidence checks and exclude manuscript-signature
verification. Sixteen associated receipt and approval-closure tests passed.

confirmation, archival service latency, independent users and clean-machine

The earlier in-memory measurements remain prototype-scale rather than a throughput claim. They

10, 100, 1,000, and 5,000 objects respectively; peak traced memory at 5,000
objects was 7.04 MiB. This excludes filesystem, COSE, and network cost, so it
is a smoke test for obvious scaling anomalies, not a statistically powered
performance evaluation; dispersion beyond minimum and median was not retained.

\section{Related Work}

The profile builds on established foundations. PROV-DM supplies a general vocabulary for entities, activities, agents, derivations, and bundles~\cite{prov}. Software Heritage identifiers provide intrinsic identifiers for software objects~\cite{swhid}; Trusty URIs provide hash-bearing immutable artifact identifiers~\cite{trusty}. COSE defines the signed binary envelope used for approvals~\cite{cose}. SCITT specifies a broader architecture for signed statements and transparency services~\cite{scitt}, while COSE Receipts standardizes receipt envelopes~\cite{cosereceipts}; neither registration nor a valid receipt makes the registered statement true. RFC~3161 specifies the timestamp protocol~\cite{rfc3161}; RFC~9921 explicitly distinguishes COSE-then-timestamp from timestamp-then-COSE~\cite{rfc9921}. ACSD uses the former ordering as a semantic analogy: its separate approval-set object is timestamped after signature collection, without claiming wire-level interoperability with the RFC~9921 header profile. TUF uses predecessor and successor root thresholds to preserve trust through root-key updates~\cite{tufspec}; in-toto binds supply-chain steps to authorized functionaries~\cite{intoto}. ACSD specializes related authorization ideas to an exact anonymous scholarly WorkID succession rule.

C2PA Content Credentials bind signed provenance assertions to media assets and
explicitly separate tamper validation from judgments about whether an assertion
is good or true~\cite{c2pa}. The CAWG Identity Assertion adds creator identity
assertions and validation results for that ecosystem~\cite{cawg}. ClaimChain
provides privacy-preserving authenticated claim repositories with
non-equivocation goals~\cite{claimchain}. These systems establish signed
provenance, scoped identity, selective disclosure, and conflict resistance as
existing design families. ACSD contributes a smaller scholarly-release profile
that evaluates approval completion, revision authority, mutual-citation
separation, a closed claim algebra, and executable counterexamples together.

SD-JWT defines selective disclosure for claims in a signed JSON payload~\cite{sdjwt}, while BBS-based verifiable-credential suites additionally target unlinkable derived proofs~\cite{bbs}. For public linkage, ACSD instead publishes a direct link from an already public release-slot key to one identity assertion. This smaller mechanism avoids an issuer/holder credential layer, while the typed claim boundary leaves unlinkable credentials as a separable service level.

A detached signature already authenticates exact bytes under a chosen public
key. Moreover, a minimal predecessor-signed transition over exact parent,
child, and successor-key bytes can reproduce ACSD's core key-rotation decision.
Our controlled baseline confirms this positive result instead of comparing only
against self-declared or permanently pinned keys. ACSD's distinction is the
integration of this edge with canonical scholarly objects, ordered and
threshold author authority, signature-complete closure, scoped disclosures,
and a closed output vocabulary.

\acsd\ combines these established mechanisms into an executable scholarly profile: self-contained anonymous releases; exact predecessor-authorized succession; a separate series layer for mutual citations; exact text-token witnesses; explicit conflict predicates; and counterexamples against common overclaims about signatures, local clocks, and copied content. Appendix~\ref{app:capability-boundary} summarizes the nearest-neighbor capability boundary.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Several extensions are intentionally deferred. First, relations across independently administered series require policy and an interoperable cross-series package model. Second, anti-equivocation evidence from transparency logs or gossip requires deployed service assumptions. Third, although Lean strictly decodes the restricted appraisal transcript, the filesystem, release-object, DER/CBOR, COSE, RFC~3161, canonicalization, hash, and signature adapters remain an explicit trusted implementation boundary rather than proof targets for this profile. Fourth, encrypted PKCS\#8 protects an exported key file at rest, but operating-system-backed storage, hardware signing, backup/migration workflows, and malware resistance remain distinct service levels. Fifth, exact TSA signer pinning does not replace PKIX path construction or revocation when a deployment requires those services. Long-lived evidence also needs an explicit renewal policy, such as renewed timestamps or an evidence-record scheme when algorithms or certificates age~\cite{ers}. Sixth, precommitted recovery avoids post-hoc takeover but does not prove guardian custody, revoke a valid historical transition, discover withheld competing children, or select a global head without transparency, pinning, gossip, or governance. Seventh, the actual-client comparison measures local GnuPG, detached Ed25519 and ACSD creation/verification costs; it does not measure OpenTimestamps or archival services, independent users, or production deployment. Eighth, scoped disclosures provide integrity and exact binding, not authenticated natural-person identity, freedom from coercion, completeness of a selectively opened research record, or resistance to social inference about undisclosed collaborators. A higher-assurance identity service could require an independently authenticated subject countersignature or credential; we deliberately exclude the resulting issuer and revocation trust model from this profile. Finally, content-anonymous publication does not hide repository-account, network, timing, or public-key linkage, and richer citation/editor integration remains future work.

These are reasons to state the present result narrowly, not reasons to weaken the useful evidence it already preserves.

\section{Conclusion}

\acsd\ turns a vague ``anonymous signed paper'' idea into an executable evidence profile. It binds exact objects to pseudonymous team approval and composes predecessor-authorized succession, exact recovery, mutual citations, fork detection, and scoped disclosures under a closed claim vocabulary. Ordinary primitives supply individual decisions; ACSD contributes their scholarly composition and modeled attack exclusions. Natural-person identity, originality, global head selection, and independent time remain separate claims.
